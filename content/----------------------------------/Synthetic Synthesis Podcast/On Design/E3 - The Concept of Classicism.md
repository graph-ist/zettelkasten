## Summary

This document synthesizes a lecture on the fundamental psychological behaviors that underpin creation, design, and cultural evolution. The central thesis posits three distinct yet often intertwined mental pathways for creating, investigating, or inventing: the **Classical** approach, which relies on referencing pre-existing models; the **Ontological** approach, an internal investigation drawing from deep-seated genetic and historical memory; and the path of **Contamination**, where innovation arises from the cross-pollination of different cultures and languages. These creative frameworks are used to analyze the crucial distinction between **[[E1 - [[E1 - Modernity and Contemporaneity in the 20th Century|Modernity]] and Contemporaneity in the 20th Century|Modernity]]**, which tends to be classical in its reference to the past, and **Contemporaneity**, which often seeks a complete break (_tabula rasa_) from history. Finally, the analysis culminates in a definition of Italian design as a holistic, utopian project aimed at redesigning the entire world, a concept traced back to the integrated cultural vision of the Counter-Reformation and Richard Wagner's _Gesamtkunstwerk_ (total work of art), distinguishing it sharply from mere object-focused **Stylism**. The document concludes by framing the contemporary challenge for creators as the need to formulate a new, overarching vision by navigating these three fundamental creative pathways.

## The Three Core Mental Approaches to Creation

The lecture identifies three parallel nuclei of psychological behavior used in the acts of looking at, investigating, and inventing things. These pathways form the fundamental grammar of creativity.

### 1. The Classical Approach: Citing an Existing Model

The classical approach is defined as any creative act that refers to a pre-existing model. The term "classico" itself derives from the Roman rector Aulus Gellius, who advised students of rhetoric to study the books of the first class (_classis_), the highest societal tier, which also supplied the elite naval forces (_classis_). Thus, "classical" fundamentally means that which refers to a supreme, pre-existing model.

- **Core Principle:** "To look forward, you need a rear-view mirror; to invent the new, you must have a precise awareness of the past." (A concept attributed to Leon Battista Alberti).
- **Historical Examples:**
    - **Emperor Hadrian:** His villa in Tivoli was a classical operation, reassembling models and experiences from his travels.
    - **Petrarch:** His longing for the perfection of Roman antiquity is identified as the first seed of the Renaissance, which itself was a revival of classical models.
    - **The Renaissance:** The rediscovery of the Laocoön statue group in the early 16th century legitimized and influenced Michelangelo's powerful, muscular sculptural style, providing a "dazzling example" from the past.
    - **Neoclassicism:** The 18th-century excavations of Herculaneum and Pompeii provided new classical models (e.g., the slender pilaster), which in turn influenced the style of the Royal Palace of Caserta and, subsequently, the Louis XVI style in France.
- **Modern Application (Picasso):** Pablo Picasso is presented as a "perennial classicist." Rather than being an artist of rupture, he constantly sought and sublimated models. When he referenced Greco-Roman sculpture, he was classical. When he referenced African sculpture—a new model appearing in Europe at the time—he was still performing a classical act, but by changing the reference, he created something entirely new.

### 2. The Ontological Approach: The Internal Path

This approach is the opposite of the classical. Instead of looking outward for models, it involves an internal investigation into one's own "ontological path of being," tapping into instinctual drives and deeply embedded memories.

- **Core Principle:** This creative drive is rooted in the idea that all humans share a sediment of ontological experiences, linking our individual being (_ontology_) with the history of our species' development (_phylogeny_). We possess a collective, historical consciousness and genetic memory.
- **Evidence and Examples:**
    - **Genetic Memory in Animals:** The lecturer cites the innate behavior of hunting dogs (a Setter or Pointer) instinctively pointing at a partridge, a behavior bred over centuries, in contrast to a poodle that would try to eat it. Similarly, a Maremmano horse instinctively fears puddles (recalling quicksand), while a French horse enjoys them.
    - **Cross-Cultural Symbols:** The appearance of identical decorative columns with superimposed lozenges in three disconnected cultures—African tribes, the Moche civilization in Latin America, and the work of Romanian sculptor Constantin Brancusi—suggests a common, internal aesthetic datum that transcends direct cultural contact.

### 3. The Approach of Contamination: The Fusion of Languages

The third behavior involves drawing references not from within one's own language or culture, but from others. Contamination is presented as a source of immense cultural wealth and a powerful engine for change.

- **Core Principle:** Cultural evolution is frequently driven by the introduction of external elements that alter perception and break established parameters. The most effective communication in art is described as a "virus" that "enters the other's head and alters their visual system."
- **Key Examples of Contamination:**
    - **Arab Influence on Europe:** The reintroduction of Aristotle's philosophy via Arab scholars, the development of mathematics (zero, algebra, algorithm), alchemy (elixir, distillation), and cuisine (sugar, saffron in Milanese risotto).
    - **The Opening of Japan (19th Century):** This is cited as one of the most powerful contaminations for the West.
        - **Fashion:** The kimono introduced the idea of free-flowing garments, beginning a revolution against the restrictive corset that would lead to modern fashion.
        - **Architecture:** The Japanese house introduced concepts of non-serial, integrated spaces and lightweight partitions, challenging 3,000 years of Western architectural parameters. The acceptance of a room's "constructive conceptuality" is a direct lesson from Japanese aesthetics.
    - **Cuisine and Identity:** Seemingly traditional identities are shown to be products of contamination.
        - **Italian Food:** The "most Italian thing in the world," the pizza, is composed of a non-native tomato, basil, and mozzarella from the buffalo, an 18th-century import. Polenta and beans are from the Americas.
        - **Milanese Food:** The _cotoletta_ derives its name from the Napoleonic French _côtelette_, and risotto alla luganega uses a sausage (_lucanica_) originating from Southern Italy.

## Modernity vs. Contemporaneity

A critical distinction is drawn between being "modern" and being "contemporary," representing two conflicting attitudes toward history.

### Modernity

Modernity tends to be classical. It positions itself on the timeline of history and engages with the past to create the new. It acknowledges a vector connecting the present to what came before.

- **Example:** Le Corbusier, while aiming to invent a new world, founded his architectural vision on inherited parameters like the golden section. He was "intimately modern" in this gesture.

### Contemporaneity

Contemporaneity seeks to sever ties with the past. Its driving impulse is the _tabula rasa_—the clean slate. This desire can lead to a forceful imposition of a new vision, abolishing alternatives.

- **Examples:**
    - **Adolf Loos:** His declaration against decoration was a call for _tabula rasa_, to invent a new world based on rationality, free from historical baggage.
    - **Political Movements:** Totalitarian movements like Bolshevism and Fascism shared the idea of creating a completely new society from scratch, annulling the past.
- **Critique:** The lecturer expresses a methodological revolt against the concept of a true _tabula rasa_, arguing that it is an illusion. Our language, our tools, and everything we produce are the result of a long sedimentation process.

## The Nature of Design versus Stylism

The lecture applies these concepts to define the unique character of Italian design, contrasting it with the more limited scope of stylism.

### Design: A Holistic and Utopian Vision

True design, particularly in the Italian tradition, is not about creating a single object but about proposing a comprehensive vision for redesigning the world. It is the ambition to create an entire, integrated environment.

- **Definition:** "Design is the transformation of a collective utopia into a possible path, a definable path."
- **Historical Lineage:** This holistic approach has deep, indigenous roots in Italian culture.
    1. **Post-WWII Milanese Designers (Ponti, Castiglioni, etc.):** This generation imagined a completely new way of living, from the building to the furniture to the small objects within, rejecting the fascist past.
    2. **Futurist Manifesto (1915):** Giacomo Balla's manifesto proposed that all elements of the universe could be shaped into a single, aggregating project.
    3. **Richard Wagner's** _**Gesamtkunstwerk**_**:** The concept of the "total work of art" where the composer controls the text, music, language, and stagecraft to create an all-encompassing experience.
    4. **The Counter-Reformation and the Baroque:** This is identified as the genetic moment of the Italian design sensibility. As a reaction against Protestant iconoclasm, the Counter-Reformation established an **equivalence of practices**. The woodcarver of a confessional held the same dignity as a great painter. This led to an integrated aesthetic where liturgy, vestments, incense, music, and architecture combined into a single, overwhelming experience—a "design operation." This "summation of opuses" is the essence of the Baroque and the origin of opera, cementing a holistic cultural sensibility in Italy.

### Stylism

Stylism is the act of creating a single, well-designed object in isolation. It lacks the overarching, world-building ambition of design. The focus is on the "well-made little thing" without concern for the world surrounding it.

- **Example:** Philip Starck is initially presented as a designer of "gags," but it is argued that even his playful, iconoclastic work ultimately proposes a coherent, if goliardic, vision of the world.

## The Present Challenge: The Absence of Vision

The lecture concludes with a critical assessment of the current state of design and a challenge to future creators.

- **The Current State:** Contemporary design is characterized as an "endless little-baroque" (_barocchetto senza fine_) and a dominant Mannerism. It is perceived as lacking a foundational utopian vision or a robust debate about what it should propose to the world.
- **The Path Forward:** The central question for creators today is: **"What vision can we imagine now?"** Any answer, the lecture concludes, must be formulated by navigating the three fundamental pathways—**classical citation**, **ontological investigation**, or **cultural contamination**. The road is open, but these three channels remain the inescapable grammar of creativity.
