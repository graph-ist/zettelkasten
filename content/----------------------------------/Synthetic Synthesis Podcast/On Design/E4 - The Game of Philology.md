Questa è la rielaborazione della quarta lezione di Rudimenti di etica per il design di [[Philippe Daverio]] che si può trovare sul canale Synthetic Synthesis.

## Summary

This document synthesizes a detailed philological and anthropological analysis of the concept of beauty, arguing that "bello" (the beautiful) is not a universal or objective truth but a fluid socio-cultural construct. The central thesis posits that our perception of beauty is determined by the specific historical, linguistic, and economic realities of a community as it seeks to define its identity.

By examining the etymology of fundamental words like "work," "luxury," and "beauty" itself across European languages, the analysis reveals deeply rooted, often contradictory, cultural values. The German concept of beauty (`schön`) is linked to the sun and brilliance, leading to a positive valuation of luxury (`lusso`). Conversely, the Roman tradition, valuing distinction (`electus`), forms the basis for elegance (`eleganza`) and views luxury as a negative.

The lecture concludes that the most enduring and useful principle for design is the ancient Greek concept of _kalòs kai agathòs_—that which is beautiful is also good and useful. This places an ethical imperative at the core of the creative process. The ultimate task for a designer is not to find an aesthetic vision, but to first build an ethical vision of the world, from which an authentic aesthetic will automatically emerge.

## 1. The Philological Method: Deconstructing Core Concepts

The lecture employs philology as a primary tool to reveal the underlying mechanisms in the formation of concepts. By tracing the etymology of everyday words across European cultures, a vast divergence in worldview becomes apparent. While some concepts, like "wine," are surprisingly uniform due to a shared cultural anchor (Christianity), others reveal fundamental differences.

|            |                           |              |                    |                      |                      |                     |                                                                                                                                              |
| ---------- | ------------------------- | ------------ | ------------------ | -------------------- | -------------------- | ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Concept    | Italian                   | French       | Spanish            | German               | English              | Russian             | Etymological/Cultural Root                                                                                                                   |
| **Wine**   | `vino`                    | `vin`        | `vino`             | `Wein`               | `wine`               | `вино (vino)`       | **Unified:** Rooted in the necessity of wine for the Christian Mass, spreading the word uniformly across Europe.                             |
| **Bread**  | `pane`                    | `pain`       | `pan`              | `Brot`               | `bread`              |                     | **Divergent:** Latin-based words refer to the object itself, while Germanic words refer to the product of the oven (`Brot`/`bread`).         |
| **Beauty** | `bello`                   | `beau`       | `lindo`, `hermoso` | `schön`              | `handsome`, `pretty` | `красота (krasota)` | **Highly Divergent:** Reveals distinct cultural values regarding gender, ethics, and aesthetics (see Section 2).                             |
| **Work**   | `lavorare`, `travagliare` | `travailler` | `trabajo`          | `Arbeit`, `schaffen` | `work`               | `работа (rabota)`   | **Highly Divergent:** Meanings range from peasant toil (`labor`), childbirth (`travagliare`), creation (`schaffen`), and slavery (`Arbeit`). |

The analysis of the word "work" is particularly revealing:

- **English** `**work**`: Has the same root as the German word for a tool (`Werkzeug`), suggesting a psychological inclination towards tool-making that fueled the [[E1 - Modernity and Contemporaneity in the 20th Century|Industrial Revolution]].
- **German** `**Arbeit**`: Is etymologically linked to the Russian word for slave (`rab`). This makes the infamous slogan `Arbeit macht frei` ("Work sets you free") profoundly cruel, as its literal root means "the slave is free."
- **Latin-based** `**travail**`**/**`**trabajo**`: Derives from the concept of childbirth (`partorire`), framing work as a generative, albeit painful, act.

## 2. The Myth of Universal Beauty

The lecture's central argument is the non-existence of a stable, objective concept of beauty. This is demonstrated through linguistic analysis and historical examples of aesthetic ideals.

### A. Linguistic Deconstruction of "Bello"

The word for "beautiful" varies radically, reflecting disparate cultural values:

- **Italian/French (**`**bello**`**/**`**beau**`**)**: Potentially derives from the medieval concept of strength and martial prowess (`bellum` - war), similar to the archaic Italian `gallo` (valiant).
- **Spanish (**`**lindo**`**/**`**hermoso**`**)**: Uses two distinct words. A woman can be `linda` (clean, washed), while a man is `hermoso` (muscular, from the Latin `formosus`), revealing separate aesthetic standards for men and women.
- **German (**`**schön**`**)**: Originates from `Sonne` (the sun). Beauty is that which is solar, warming, and radiant, implying a completely different ethical dimension.
- **English (**`**handsome**`**/**`**pretty**`**/**`**beautiful**`**)**: Possesses a series of parallel, non-interchangeable terms. `Handsome` relates to strength, `pretty` to being cute, and `beautiful` is an import from Old French.

### B. Misinterpreting St. Augustine

The popular phrase "Beauty will save the world," often attributed to Pope John Paul II, is identified as a misreading of St. Augustine.

- Augustine never used a general term for "beauty." For ancient Romans, a man was `formosus` (well-formed, muscular) while only a girl could be described with `pulchritudo`. To call a Roman legionary `pulcher` would be an insult.
- Augustine wrote that _pulchritudo_ would save the world, referring specifically to the grace of God, not to aesthetic beauty. This concept of beauty-as-grace was later adopted and adapted by Dostoevsky in _The Idiot_.

### C. The Shifting Ideal of the Human Form

The ideal human body, particularly the female form, is presented as radically unstable, changing to reflect prevailing socio-economic conditions.

- **Renaissance (Botticelli)**: The ideal was slender and athletic, reflecting the rediscovery of Greco-Roman antiquity and the active life of Italian city-states.
- **Baroque (Rubens, Rembrandt)**: The ideal became overweight, with an "exultation of cellulite." In an age of widespread famine and war, being fat was a visible declaration of wealth and health. "Fat is rich, and rich is beautiful."
- **Early 20th Century**: The ideal shifted to extreme thinness, encapsulated in Wallis Simpson's maxim, "You can never be too rich or too thin."
- **Post-World War II**: The "maggiorate" (curvaceous, prosperous women) became the ideal. After years of wartime starvation, a prosperous and healthy appearance was considered the most beautiful.

## 3. Opposing Cultural Frameworks: Luxury vs. Elegance

The analysis presents luxury (`lusso`) and elegance (`eleganza`) not as synonyms, but as fundamentally opposed concepts originating from different cultural worldviews.

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|Concept|**Luxury (**`**Lusso**`**)**|**Elegance (**`**Eleganza**`**)**|
|**Origin**|Germanic culture|Roman culture|
|**Etymology**|Rooted in `lux` (light) and the Germanic `schön` (from `Sonne`, sun). Beauty is that which shines and glitters.|Rooted in `electus` (the chosen one), from the act of being elected or chosen from a list.|
|**Core Value**|Expenditure, material brilliance, economic power. A positive value.|Distinction, belonging, class of power. Non-expenditure.|
|**Historical Example**|Germanic barbarians melting Roman gold coins to forge ornate belt buckles (`fibula`), a direct display of wealth on the body.|A Roman senator wearing a simple white toga with a single red stripe—a mark of status, not wealth.|
|**Roman View**|Luxury was seen as a moral negative, to the point that laws (`lex sumptuaria`) were enacted to prohibit excessive spending.|Elegance was the mark of the ruling class.|
|**Modern Manifestation**|Conspicuous consumption, ornate and glittering design. The "Russian taste" for gold-plated furniture.|Understated design, minimalism, a sense of belonging to an elite.|

This fundamental opposition—luxury to dominate through economic power versus elegance to be dominant through distinction—remains a core parameter in modern design and marketing.

## 4. The Foundational Principle: _Kalòs kai Agathòs_ (The Beautiful and The Good)

Amidst the relativity of aesthetic concepts, the lecture identifies the ancient Greek principle of _kalòs kai agathòs_ as the most enduring and useful foundation for design.

- **The Principle:** In Greek thought, `kalos` (beautiful) does not exist in isolation. An object is beautiful _because_ it is also `agathos` (good, useful, morally right).
- **Utility Defines Beauty:** The core idea is that an object is beautiful if it serves its purpose well.
    - **The Horse Example:** A horse judged to have the "best model" (form) is almost always the one with the smoothest, most efficient gallop. Its beauty derives from its functional perfection, refined over 18,000 years of human interaction.
    - **The Armchair Example:** A Louis XV armchair, with its rigid and upright posture, seems uncomfortable by modern standards. However, it was perfectly designed for its user: a 18th-century cavalry officer who spent his life on horseback and needed to maintain that posture ("heels down, back straight"). The chair was `agathos` (good/useful) for him, and therefore it was `kalos` (beautiful).

## 5. The Social Chromatics of Style

The choice between monochrome and polychrome palettes is not merely aesthetic but is deeply tied to the social structure of a given era.

- **Monochrome (Black, Grey, White)**: This is the palette of **democracy and social integration**. It allows newly ascendant social classes to participate in society without making aesthetic errors that would betray their origins.
    - **Examples**: The black suits of post-revolutionary France, the black-clad but wealthy Dutch merchants of the 17th century, Henry Ford's democratic Model T ("any color...so long as it is black"), and the minimalist grey/black uniforms of designers like Armani and Prada.
- **Polychrome (Varied, Bright Colors)**: This is the palette of **aristocracy and classism**. It requires a sophisticated, pre-existing code of taste to be used correctly, thereby functioning as a barrier to entry for the uninitiated.
    - **Examples**: The hyper-chromatic dress of the 18th-century French court, the eclectic interiors of the Belle Époque, and the challenging color schemes of Ettore Sottsass's Memphis Group, which functioned as an exclusive "initiatory rite."

## 6. Conclusion: An Ethical Foundation for Modern Design

The lecture culminates in a powerful conclusion for contemporary designers. Having systematically deconstructed the idea of a universal "bello," it proposes a new foundation for creativity.

The popular wisdom that "it's not what is beautiful that is beautiful, but what is liked that is beautiful" (`non è bello ciò che è bello, ma è bello ciò che piace`) is refined: "what is liked" is simply that which conforms to the socio-cultural identity a specific group creates for itself at a specific moment.

Therefore, the designer's true challenge is not aesthetic, but ethical. The final, overarching principle is:

**"A vision of the world generates the vision of the object. Your real problem is not to find an aesthetic vision; your main problem is to have an ethical vision. You must first build a vision of the world, and all the rest will follow automatically."**

This calls for designers to move beyond established canons and explore new pathways rooted in contemporary ethical concerns, such as:

- **Frugality and Recycling**: Finding beauty and utility in discarded materials, as exemplified by the artist who compacts discarded flip-flops from the ocean into a new material.
- **Cross-Cultural Contamination**: Seeking new aesthetic referents beyond the Western tradition, as Sottsass did by looking to India.
- **Reinventing Beauty for New Contexts**: Addressing the aesthetic needs of all members of society, not just the elite, by imagining how to create a beautiful and functional space with minimal resources.

Ultimately, the ancient principle of _kalòs kai agathòs_ is reaffirmed for the 21st century: the design process must begin with defining what is useful and good (`agathos`) for the world today, from which true beauty (`kalos`) will emerge.
