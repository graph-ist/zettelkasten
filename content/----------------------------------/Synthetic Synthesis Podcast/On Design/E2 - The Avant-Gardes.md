## Summary

The concept of the avant-garde, a term often misappropriated, is fundamental to understanding the history of [[E1 - Modernity and Contemporaneity in the 20th Century|[[E1 - Modernity and Contemporaneity in the 20th Century|modernity]]]]. Its most accurate definition stems not from politics but from the military tactics of Julius Caesar: a small, specialized nucleus sent ahead of the main body of troops to illuminate the path and absorb the initial risk. This establishes the avant-garde's core identity—an entity that exists only in a dialectical relationship with "the masses," a large, organized collective moving with a unified direction or "vector." The rise of the avant-garde in the 19th and 20th centuries was therefore intrinsically linked to the formation of modern, industrial masses.

However, the contemporary postmodern era is characterized by the dissolution of these masses. The unified vector has fragmented into a multitude of individual trajectories, rendering the traditional avant-garde model obsolete. In its place, new paradigms of cultural influence have emerged, bearing a striking resemblance to medieval archetypes. These include the "Tribune," a popular orator who articulates the sentiments of a transversal populace, and the elite, solitary "Lancelot," whose advanced, personal quests influence culture through slow osmosis rather than direct leadership. Creativity today operates within this new framework, navigating a landscape of historical suspension where all past eras feel contemporary. It draws upon three primary impulses: **Classicism** (innovation through historical reference), **Romanticism** (creation from an internal source), and **Contamination** (transformation through external influence).

## 1. Redefining the Avant-Garde

The term "avant-garde" has been distorted to the point of becoming academic or routine. A precise definition is essential to grasp its role in modern history. The concept's origins are found in both political and military discourse, with the latter providing the most potent and enduring model.

### Political Origins: The Lenin-Trotsky Debate

The first significant intellectual debates on the avant-garde occurred in the political sphere, centered on the opposing views of Lenin and Trotsky regarding the leadership of the working class.

| Figure      | Thesis on the Avant-Garde                                                                       | Core Rationale                                                                                                    | Subsequent Critique                                                                                           |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Lenin**   | The avant-garde must be **internal**to the working class.                                       | It must be a revolutionary reality generated from within the class itself to pull it towards historical currents. | -                                                                                                             |
| **Trotsky** | The avant-garde must be **external** to the working class, composed primarily of intellectuals. | An alienated working class is incapable of generating its own vanguard.                                           | The Stalinist critique argued that if the vanguard is external, it is necessarily bourgeois and thus invalid. |

This political debate, which ended tragically for its participants, framed the avant-garde as a force of societal leadership and transformation.

### The Definitive Model: Julius Caesar's Legions

The most accurate and illuminating definition of the avant-garde comes from Julius Caesar's _De Bello Gallico_.

- **The Action:** Caesar would select a small group—a centurion and a few soldiers—and send them ahead of the main legions for reconnaissance.
- **The Role:** This "avanguardia" was tasked with exploring the terrain and assessing the enemy.
- **The Risk:** These groups often met a "bad end," being "returned broken and plucked."
- **The Core Concept:** This military strategy establishes the foundational principles of the avant-garde:
    1. **Dialectical Relationship:** An avant-garde only exists in relation to "il grosso delle truppe" (the main body of troops) that follows. Without the mass, the concept is meaningless.
    2. **Function:** Its purpose is to illuminate the path forward for the mass and drag it in a specific direction.
    3. **Motivation:** Members of the artistic avant-garde are not in search of immediate success for themselves, but rather a new path that will eventually become successful for the masses.

## 2. The Necessary Condition: The Rise and Fall of the Masses

The existence of the avant-garde is entirely contingent on the existence of "the masses." This term does not refer to a general populace but to a specific societal formation characteristic of the modern era.

### The Modern "Mass": A Unified Vector

- **Definition:** Masses are large, organized segments of a population that share a single, univocal direction, creating a powerful societal "vector."
- **Historical Example:** The sight of 10,000 bicycles in front of factories in Lecco at 7:00 AM. All workers moved in the same direction at the same time, forming a mass with a clear directional vector that could be influenced or "dragged."
- **Pre-Modern Era:** The Middle Ages did not have masses; it had "transversal peoples" without such organized, singular direction.

### The Postmodern Condition: Fragmentation and the End of the Mass

- **The Shift:** The contemporary world is defined by the disappearance of the mass. The unified vector has ceased to exist. At 7:00 AM today, individuals move in myriad directions; the "vector summation" results in a standstill.
- **Catalyst of Change:** Technological innovation, particularly in communication, has been a primary driver of this fragmentation. The lecturer cites a personal anecdote from the 1970s of using an early car phone while stuck in traffic. This ability to communicate with Berlin from a traffic jam in Milan represented an escape from the mass—a breaking of the collective, directional experience.
- **The Consequence:** The absence of a unified mass to lead or pull makes the traditional function of an avant-garde impossible. This is the central crisis of the avant-garde concept and a defining feature of postmodernity.

## 3. A History of Avant-Garde Movements

From the 19th century onwards, the dynamic between avant-garde nuclei and the masses shaped art, culture, and politics.

- **The Genesis: Hugo's** _**Hernani**_ **(1830):** The premiere of Victor Hugo's play in Paris is identified as the first powerful phenomenon of a successful avant-garde. A small group of young intellectuals and artists (including Théophile Gautier) created a public clash with the older generation. This artistic rupture captured the city's attention, leading to hundreds of subsequent performances for over 150,000 people. Five months later, the same energies fueled the July Revolution, demonstrating how a micro-nucleus could drag a mass toward radical political change.
- **Ideological Nuclei: The Macchiaioli and the Parisian Salons:**
    - **The Macchiaioli (Florence, 1850s):** In the liberal environment of pre-unification Florence, a group of artists gathered at Caffè Michelangelo. Knowing their work would not sell, they nonetheless formed a new aesthetic identity that would have a radical influence on the entire Italian peninsula.
    - **The Parisian Salons:** The official, academic Salon created a power structure. In reaction, alternative venues like the _Salon des Refusés_ (Salon of the Rejected) and the _Salon des Indépendants_ were formed. These became the incubators for Impressionism, acting as an oppositional force that aimed to pull the public in a new direction.
- **Visions of the World: The Pre-Raphaelites, Morris, and Dresser:**
    - In London, the **Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood** formed as an alternative to the conservative Royal Academy.
    - This movement was not merely aesthetic but proposed an alternative vision of life. **William Morris** emerged from this circle, launching a critique of industrial London by advocating for a return to craftsmanship, Celtic roots, and medieval myths.
    - In direct opposition was **Christopher Dresser**, who embraced industry and sought to improve and rationalize it.
    - This dichotomy (cf. [[William Morris vs Christopher Dresser]]) — Morris's revolutionary rupture versus Dresser's rationalization of existing power—defines the central debate in design and architecture throughout modernity.

### Core Characteristics of the Avant-Garde

1. **Ideological Nature:** They are not just proposing a new aesthetic but a new vision of the world.
2. **External Position:** They operate as small groups, separate from the mass they intend to influence.
3. **Conflictual Dialogue:** Their relationship with the masses is often confrontational. The Futurists giving "slaps to the public" is a prime example.
4. **Aversion to Success:** They do not initially seek success. If they achieve it, they cease to be an avant-garde and become the establishment.

## 4. The Post-Avant-Garde Era: A Neo-Medieval Paradigm

With the disappearance of the masses, the avant-garde model has been replaced by two new archetypes of influence that echo the social dynamics of the Middle Ages.

### The Tribune: The Voice of the Transversal Populace

- **The Role:** A public figure, akin to a medieval preacher, who articulates what the "transversal populace" already thinks, but in a more refined or powerful language ("a better Latin"). They are popularizers and provocateurs.
- **Modern Examples:** The text cites figures like Vittorio Sgarbi and designer Philippe Starck as contemporary "tribunes."

### The Elite Explorer: The "Lancelot" Model

- **The Role:** An elite creator or thinker who embarks on a sophisticated, solitary quest, much like Lancelot seeking the Holy Grail.
- **Motivation:** They have no intention of dragging the masses along; in fact, the masses would be an "annoying encumbrance." Their journey is personal and esoteric.
- **Influence:** Despite their isolation, their work is not sterile. The ideas developed in these elite "laboratories" influence the broader culture through a slow process of "osmosis" and contamination.
- **Modern Examples:** The world of theoretical physics (Roger Penrose) and advanced visual arts (Massimo Vignelli) operate in this mode, creating concepts that eventually trickle down and reshape mainstream thought.

## 5. The Three Fundamental Creative Impulses

Creativity, whether in an avant-garde or post-avant-garde context, is driven by a combination of three fundamental behaviors or approaches.

1. **Classicism: Innovation Through Reference:**
    - **Mechanism:** Using an external example—often from the past—as a reference point to break with the present. The term "classic" derives from the Roman _classis_, or first class, signifying a model of excellence.
    - **Examples:** Petrarca used Roman antiquity as a model to break from the medieval culture he rejected. Picasso was a consummate classicist, finding his references first in African sculpture to invent Cubism, and later in Roman art after visiting Italy. His motto encapsulates this approach: _"Io non cerco, io trovo"_ ("I don't seek, I find").
2. **Romanticism: The Internal, Visceral Source ("Sorgivo"):**
    - **Mechanism:** Rejecting external models to draw inspiration from a deep, internal, visceral source. This approach aims to be "primo" (first), not chronologically, but in its authentic expression.
    - **Underlying Principle:** This impulse taps into a common, phylogenetic human heritage. The similarities between a Brancusi sculpture and primitive art from different continents arise not from copying but from accessing a shared "collective consciousness." This is creation based on ontology (the nature of being).
3. **Contamination: Transformation Through Influence:**
    - **Mechanism:** Creation that arises from being altered by external cultural contact. It is not a conscious reference but an absorbed influence that changes one's way of seeing and being.
    - **Examples:** Western culture has been fundamentally contaminated by Arab culture (introducing concepts like distillation and algorithms) and later by Japanese culture. The arrival of Japanese art in Europe introduced aesthetics of flat color and minimalism, without which the pronouncements of figures like Adolf Loos ("Ornament and Crime") would be unthinkable.

## 6. The Contemporary Condition: Historical Suspension and Information Saturation

The current era is defined by a unique relationship with history and information, posing new challenges for creativity.

- **The End of Historical Dialectics:** The relentless, conflict-driven progression that defined modernity appears to have paused. Fashions and styles no longer change with generational ruptures; an outfit from 1950 can appear contemporary, something unthinkable in previous centuries. The youth, for the first time since 1789, no longer stage revolutions.
- **The "Imaginary Museum": Everything is Contemporary:** As theorized by André Malraux before the internet, technology has given us access to the entire history of global culture simultaneously. A Khmer statue and a Catalan Christ are no longer separated by time and space but exist in relation to one another on a screen. We live in a vast, non-linear "koinè" (common language) where a 170-year-old Thonet chair and a 90-year-old Mies van der Rohe chair feel equally present and relevant.
- **The Creative Challenge:** The primary task for a creator today is no longer to form a vanguard to lead a nonexistent mass. Instead, it is to navigate this immense, contemporary "magma" of information. Creativity must emerge from a dialogue between the refined, elite experiments of the "Lancelots" and the transversal populace, drawing from the deep impulses of classicism, romanticism, and contamination to forge meaning in an era of unprecedented freedom and complexity.
